Where does orientation selectivity come from?

Question

A neuron in primary visual cortex may respond strongly to one edge orientation and weakly to another. Where does that preference enter the computation: in its inputs, in the conversion from input to spikes, or in the surrounding circuit?

What is selectivity?

Show the same neuron a series of striped patterns, changing only the angle of the bars. It may respond strongly to some orientations and weakly to others. This difference is what we mean by orientation selectivity. The orientation that produces the strongest response is its preferred orientation.

Where does the input come from?

A neuron receives input from different parts of visual space. How these inputs are arranged can give it a preference for one orientation. Hubel and Wiesel proposed spatial convergence as an explanation for this preference. [1]

Here we use an elongated Gaussian weighting field. Inputs near its center carry more weight. The ellipse shows its shape, while the weights taper smoothly beyond the outline.

When a bright bar follows the long axis, nearby inputs reinforce one another. Across the field, bright and dark bands contribute opposite signs and partly cancel. Each location contributes its contrast multiplied by its weight, and these contributions are added together. The right image shows the weighted contributions, with gray representing zero.

How does input become a response?

An orientation away from the preferred one can still provide input. That input may be too weak to produce spikes. A threshold gives us a simple way to describe this step. In cat visual cortex, thresholding helped explain why spike responses were more selective than the underlying membrane potential. [2]

Keep the input from the elongated field and add a threshold. Below it, the output is zero. Above it, only the amount exceeding the threshold contributes to the output.

\(\displaystyle \text{output}=\max\!\left(0,\,\text{input}-\text{threshold}\right)\)

Population dynamics

So far, each neuron has responded to its own visual input. Now place it among neurons that prefer different orientations. Their activity becomes another source of input, so a neuron's response depends on both the stimulus and the surrounding population.

We arrange the neurons by their preferred orientation. In this model, neurons with similar preferences support one another, while neurons with more distant preferences can reduce one another's input. These signed connections describe the net effect of the circuit. This approach follows classical models of orientation tuning. [3]

Connections depend on preference difference

Let \(\Delta=\varphi_i-\varphi_j\) be the difference between two neurons' preferred orientations. We choose a smooth connection profile that is strongest at zero difference and weakest when the preferences are perpendicular.

\(\displaystyle J(\Delta)=g\,[J_0+J_2\cos(2\Delta)]\)

\(J_0\) shifts the whole curve up or down, \(J_2\) sets how much it varies with preference difference, and \(g\) scales its strength. Here \(J_0=0.3\) and \(J_2=1.2\). The factor of two gives the curve a 180° period, matching the repetition of bar orientation.

Near zero, the connection is positive, so activity adds input to a neuron with a similar preference. Near ±90°, it is negative and reduces input. Moving left or right gives the same strength because only the size of the orientation difference matters.

Dynamics

A connection contributes \(W_{ij}r_j\), where \(r_j\) is the sending neuron's activity. Its weight is \(W_{ij}=J(\varphi_i-\varphi_j)/N\) for a population of \(N\) neurons. Dividing by \(N\) keeps the summed input comparable across population sizes. We set self-connections to zero.

Each neuron combines these contributions with its visual input, then applies the threshold. This gives its current target response.

\(\displaystyle R_i=\max\!\left(0,\,I_i+\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}r_j-T\right)\)

\(I_i\) is the visual input, \(\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}r_j\) is the input from the rest of the population, and \(T\) is the threshold. The activity \(r_i\) moves toward this target \(R_i\) over time.

\(\displaystyle \tau\frac{dr_i}{dt}=R_i-r_i\)

Activity rises when the target exceeds the current response and falls when it is lower. The time constant \(\tau\) sets the pace, here 20 ms. As each neuron's activity changes, the input it sends to other neurons changes too, so their targets keep changing together.

Where does the activity settle?

In the demo above, the population approaches a fixed response while the stimulus stays unchanged. The neurons remain active and continue sending input to one another. What disappears is the difference between each neuron's current activity and the target supported by its inputs. This is a steady state.

At that point, \(dr_i/dt=0\), so \(r_i^*=R_i^*\). Substituting the target response gives

\(\displaystyle r_i^*=\max\!\left(0,\,I_i+\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}r_j^*-T\right)\)

The star marks a steady-state value. \(I_i\) is the fixed visual input, \(W_{ij}\) is the connection weight, and \(T\) is the threshold. Each neuron's activity appears on the left as a response and on the right as an input to other neurons. The whole population must satisfy these relationships together.

With no connections, the population settles at the thresholded visual input. With connections, activity feeds back into the circuit and changes the final response. In the example above, positive feedback increases activity near the preferred orientation, while negative contributions suppress more distant preferences. The final shape depends on both the visual input and the connections.

This brings us back to orientation selectivity. Spatial pooling makes some orientations produce stronger input. The threshold shapes how that input becomes a response. Population interactions then reshape the activity supported by the circuit. The response we observe is the outcome of these steps working together.

Mathematical details

\(\displaystyle s_\theta(\mathbf{x})=C\cos\!\left(2\pi f\,\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}+\psi\right)\)

\(s_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) gives the signed contrast at position \(\mathbf{x}\) relative to mean luminance. The bar angle \(\theta\) determines the unit normal \(\mathbf{n}_\theta\), so \(\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}\) measures distance across the bars. Spatial frequency \(f\) sets the spacing, phase \(\psi\) shifts the pattern, and \(C\) sets its contrast.

\(\displaystyle q(\theta)=C\cos\psi\,e^{-k^2\sigma_\perp^2/2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{k^2}{2}(\sigma_\parallel^2-\sigma_\perp^2)\sin^2(\theta-\varphi)\right]\)

\(q(\theta)\) is the input obtained by weighting the grating across space, before applying the threshold. Here \(k=2\pi f\), \(\varphi\) is the angle of the field's long axis, and \(\sigma_\parallel,\sigma_\perp\) are the Gaussian widths along and across that axis. With a bright bar centered on the field and \(\sigma_\parallel\gt\sigma_\perp\), the input is largest when the bars align with the long axis.

Deriving the grating

Measure distance across the bars

A grating repeats across its bars and stays constant along them. We therefore need one coordinate that measures how far a point lies across the pattern.

Write a position as \(\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\), with \(x\) pointing right and \(y\) pointing up. Both coordinates are measured in degrees of visual angle. Let \(\theta\) be the angle of the bars, measured counterclockwise from horizontal. A unit vector along the bars is \(\mathbf{b}_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\). Rotating it by 90° gives a unit normal.

\(\displaystyle \mathbf{n}_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\)

Projecting position onto this normal gives the signed distance \(\ell\) across the bars.

\(\displaystyle \ell=\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}=-x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta\)

Turn distance into a repeating pattern

Let \(\lambda\) be the distance between neighboring bright peaks, measured across the bars. The spatial frequency \(f=1/\lambda\) counts cycles per degree of visual angle. A distance \(\ell\) therefore spans \(f\ell\) cycles. One cosine cycle takes \(2\pi\) radians, so the corresponding angle is \(2\pi f\ell\).

\(\displaystyle \cos(2\pi f\ell),\qquad \cos\!\bigl(2\pi f(\ell+\lambda)\bigr)=\cos(2\pi f\ell+2\pi)=\cos(2\pi f\ell)\)

Increasing \(f\) reduces \(\lambda\), bringing the bars closer together. At \(\ell=0\), the cosine is 1; half a spacing away it is −1; one full spacing away it returns to 1. Choosing a cosine makes the transitions between bright and dark smooth.

Set phase and contrast

A phase \(\psi\) changes where the bright peaks fall without changing their spacing or orientation. We add it to the cosine's angle.

Multiply the cosine by a contrast amplitude \(C\), with \(0\le C\le1\). The resulting signed contrast \(s_\theta\) ranges from \(-C\) to \(C\). Substituting the projected distance gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}s_\theta(\mathbf{x})&=C\cos(2\pi f\ell+\psi)\\&=C\cos\!\bigl(2\pi f(-x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta)+\psi\bigr).\end{aligned}\)

Writing \(k=2\pi f\), the same expression becomes \(s_\theta(\mathbf{x})=C\cos(k\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}+\psi)\). Here \(k\) is angular spatial frequency in radians per degree of visual angle. For horizontal bars, \(\theta=0\) and \(\ell=y\); for vertical bars, \(\theta=90^\circ\) and \(\ell=-x\).

Recover actual luminance

Signed contrast describes a deviation from a mean, not a negative amount of light. Let \(\bar L\gt0\) be the mean luminance over a complete cycle. Define the local relative deviation as \(s=(L-\bar L)/\bar L\). Multiplying by \(\bar L\) and adding the mean back gives

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\bar Ls&=L-\bar L,\\L_\theta(\mathbf{x})&=\bar L\,[1+s_\theta(\mathbf{x})]\\&=\bar L\,[1+C\cos(k\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}+\psi)].\end{aligned}\)

The cosine averages to zero over a full cycle, so the mean remains \(\bar L\). Its extreme values give \(L_{\max}=\bar L(1+C)\) and \(L_{\min}=\bar L(1-C)\). Thus the Michelson contrast of this grating is exactly \(C\).

\(\displaystyle \frac{L_{\max}-L_{\min}}{L_{\max}+L_{\min}}=\frac{2\bar LC}{2\bar L}=C\)

At \(C=0\), the image is uniform at \(\bar L\). At \(C=1\), its darkest points reach zero and its brightest points reach \(2\bar L\).

The grating in one expression

\(\displaystyle s_\theta(\mathbf{x})=C\cos\!\left(2\pi f\,\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}+\psi\right)\)

At each position \(\mathbf{x}\), this gives the signed contrast relative to mean luminance. The orientation \(\theta\) sets the direction of the bars, \(f\) sets their spacing, \(\psi\) shifts their position, and \(C\) sets the amplitude of the light–dark variation. The projection \(\mathbf{n}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{x}\) measures distance across the bars.

From an elliptical input field to orientation tuning

Describe the spatial weights

The ellipse describes how the neuron weights locations in the visual field. We model those weights with an elongated Gaussian centered at the origin. Let \(\varphi\) be its long-axis angle. Coordinates along and across the field are

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}u&=x\cos\varphi+y\sin\varphi,\\v&=-x\sin\varphi+y\cos\varphi.\end{aligned}\)

The widths \(\sigma_\parallel\ge\sigma_\perp\gt0\) are measured in degrees of visual angle. The weight decreases smoothly with distance along either axis.

\(\displaystyle w(u,v)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_\parallel\sigma_\perp}\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2\sigma_\parallel^2}-\frac{v^2}{2\sigma_\perp^2}\right)\)

This is a product of two normalized one-dimensional Gaussians, \(w(u,v)=g_{\sigma_\parallel}(u)g_{\sigma_\perp}(v)\), where

\(\displaystyle g_\sigma(z)=\frac{e^{-z^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma},\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_\sigma(z)\,dz=1\)

Write the weighted input

At each location, multiply the signed stimulus contrast by its weight, then add over space. Call the resulting external drive \(q(\theta)\).

\(\displaystyle q(\theta)=\iint_{\mathbb R^2}w(x,y)\,s_\theta(x,y)\,dx\,dy\)

Write \(\delta=\theta-\varphi\) for the orientation difference and keep \(k=2\pi f\) from the grating expression. In the field's coordinates,

\(\displaystyle x=u\cos\varphi-v\sin\varphi,\qquad y=u\sin\varphi+v\cos\varphi,\qquad dx\,dy=du\,dv\)

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\mathbf n_\theta\cdot\mathbf x &=-x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta\\ &=-\sin\theta(u\cos\varphi-v\sin\varphi)+\cos\theta(u\sin\varphi+v\cos\varphi)\\ &=u\sin(\varphi-\theta)+v\cos(\theta-\varphi)\\ &=-u\sin\delta+v\cos\delta.\end{aligned}\)

\(\displaystyle q(\theta)=C\iint g_{\sigma_\parallel}(u)g_{\sigma_\perp}(v)\cos(-ku\sin\delta+kv\cos\delta+\psi)\,du\,dv\)

Separate the two spatial directions

First integrate along \(u\), treating \(v\) as fixed.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\cos(-ku\sin\delta+kv\cos\delta+\psi) &=\cos(ku\sin\delta)\cos(kv\cos\delta+\psi)\\ &\quad+\sin(ku\sin\delta)\sin(kv\cos\delta+\psi).\end{aligned}\)

The sine term is odd in \(u\), so it vanishes under the even Gaussian weight.

\(\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\sigma_\parallel}(u)\sin(ku\sin\delta)\,du=0\)

\(\displaystyle q(\theta)=C\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\sigma_\parallel}(u)\cos(ku\sin\delta)\,du\right]\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\sigma_\perp}(v)\cos(kv\cos\delta+\psi)\,dv\right]\)

Now expand the remaining phase and integrate along \(v\).

\(\displaystyle \cos(kv\cos\delta+\psi)=\cos(kv\cos\delta)\cos\psi-\sin(kv\cos\delta)\sin\psi\)

\(\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\sigma_\perp}(v)\sin(kv\cos\delta)\,dv=0\)

\(\displaystyle q(\theta)=C\cos\psi\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\sigma_\parallel}(u)\cos(ku\sin\delta)\,du\right]\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\sigma_\perp}(v)\cos(kv\cos\delta)\,dv\right]\)

Evaluate the Gaussian average

Both factors have the same form. Evaluate it once, with \(c\) standing for either \(k\sin\delta\) or \(k\cos\delta\).

\(\displaystyle F_\sigma(c)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_\sigma(z)\cos(cz)\,dz\)

Differentiate with respect to \(c\), keeping \(\sigma\) fixed.

\(\displaystyle F_\sigma'(c)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\,g_\sigma(z)\sin(cz)\,dz\)

Differentiating the Gaussian gives \(g_\sigma'(z)=-z\,g_\sigma(z)/\sigma^2\). Substituting \(z\,g_\sigma(z)=-\sigma^2g_\sigma'(z)\) and integrating by parts yields

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}F_\sigma'(c)&=\sigma^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_\sigma'(z)\sin(cz)\,dz\\ &=\sigma^2\left[g_\sigma(z)\sin(cz)\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\sigma^2c\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_\sigma(z)\cos(cz)\,dz\\ &=-\sigma^2cF_\sigma(c).\end{aligned}\)

The boundary term vanishes because the Gaussian tends to zero. Separate variables and integrate both sides.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\frac{dF_\sigma}{dc}&=-\sigma^2cF_\sigma,\\\frac{dF_\sigma}{F_\sigma}&=-\sigma^2c\,dc,\\\int\frac{dF_\sigma}{F_\sigma}&=-\sigma^2\int c\,dc,\\\ln|F_\sigma(c)|&=-\frac{\sigma^2c^2}{2}+\text{constant}.\end{aligned}\)

At \(c=0\), the cosine is 1, so \(F_\sigma(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_\sigma(z)\,dz=1\). Substituting this initial value determines the constant and the positive sign.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\text{constant}&=\ln 1=0,\\F_\sigma(c)&=e^{-\sigma^2c^2/2}.\end{aligned}\)

The input as a function of orientation

Apply the Gaussian integral to the long and short axes.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}q(\theta)&=C\cos\psi\,e^{-k^2\sigma_\parallel^2\sin^2\delta/2}\,e^{-k^2\sigma_\perp^2\cos^2\delta/2}\\ &=C\cos\psi\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{k^2}{2}\left(\sigma_\parallel^2\sin^2\delta+\sigma_\perp^2\cos^2\delta\right)\right].\end{aligned}\)

Substitute \(\cos^2\delta=1-\sin^2\delta\) and restore \(\delta=\theta-\varphi\) to show the dependence on bar orientation directly.

\(\displaystyle q(\theta)=C\cos\psi\,e^{-k^2\sigma_\perp^2/2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{k^2}{2}(\sigma_\parallel^2-\sigma_\perp^2)\sin^2(\theta-\varphi)\right]\)

For the centered bright bars in the main demo, \(\psi=0\) and \(C\gt0\). Input is largest when the bars follow the long axis, \(\theta=\varphi\). A larger difference \(\sigma_\parallel^2-\sigma_\perp^2\) makes input fall more strongly as the bars rotate away. When the widths are equal, the orientation-dependent factor is 1 and there is no orientation preference. This is the pooled input before the threshold is applied.

Solving the population steady state

Set the time derivative to zero

For a fixed stimulus orientation \(\theta\), neuron \(i\) receives the pooled input derived above, with its own field angle \(\varphi_i\).

\(\displaystyle I_i=C\cos\psi\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{k^2}{2}\left(\sigma_\parallel^2\sin^2(\theta-\varphi_i)+\sigma_\perp^2\cos^2(\theta-\varphi_i)\right)\right]\)

The demo uses \(N=72\) equally spaced preferences, \(T=0.3\), and the connection weights

\(\displaystyle W_{ij}=\frac{g}{N}\left[0.3+1.2\cos\!\bigl(2(\varphi_i-\varphi_j)\bigr)\right]\quad(i\ne j),\qquad W_{ii}=0\)

At a steady state, denoted by a star, every time derivative is zero.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}\tau\frac{dr_i}{dt}&=-r_i+\max\!\left(0,I_i+\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}r_j-T\right),\\0&=-r_i^*+\max\!\left(0,I_i+\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}r_j^*-T\right),\\r_i^*&=\max\!\left(0,I_i+\sum_{j\ne i}W_{ij}r_j^*-T\right).\end{aligned}\)

The time constant drops out of this condition. It controls the speed of adjustment, not the steady response.

Solve for the active neurons

Let \(\mathcal A\) contain the neurons with \(r_i^*\gt0\). All other neurons have zero activity. For an active neuron, the argument of the maximum is positive, so

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}r_i^*&=I_i-T+\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}W_{ij}r_j^*,\\r_i^*-\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}W_{ij}r_j^*&=I_i-T\qquad(i\in\mathcal A).\end{aligned}\)

Collect these equations into a matrix. \(\mathbf r_{\mathcal A}^*\) and \(\mathbf I_{\mathcal A}\) contain the active neurons' rates and visual inputs; \(W_{\mathcal A\mathcal A}\) contains their mutual connections. \(\mathrm{Id}\) is the identity matrix and \(\mathbf1\) is a vector of ones.

\(\displaystyle \begin{aligned}(\mathrm{Id}-W_{\mathcal A\mathcal A})\mathbf r_{\mathcal A}^*&=\mathbf I_{\mathcal A}-T\mathbf1,\\\mathbf r_{\mathcal A}^*&=(\mathrm{Id}-W_{\mathcal A\mathcal A})^{-1}(\mathbf I_{\mathcal A}-T\mathbf1).\end{aligned}\)

When the matrix is invertible, this gives a candidate solution. A proposed active set is valid only if its solution also satisfies both threshold conditions.

\(\displaystyle \begin{cases}r_i^*\gt0,&i\in\mathcal A,\\I_i+\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}W_{ij}r_j^*-T\le0,&i\notin\mathcal A.\end{cases}\)

The second condition matters because a neuron assigned zero activity must not receive enough net input to become active. Solving the unthresholded equations for every neuron would miss this distinction.
